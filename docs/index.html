Fecal microbiota transplantation for pediatric Clostridioides difficile infection: efficacy, challenges, and the path to next-generation microbiome therapies.Review★ Gut microbes  |  2026-11-16

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is an increasing cause of morbidity in children, with recurrent disease posing substantial therapeutic challenges. Antibiotic therapy remains the cornerstone of initial management; however, up to 30% of children experience recurrence, and those with underlying comorbidities face recurrence rates as high as 65%. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has demonstrated superior efficacy over antibiotics for recurrent CDI (rCDI) in adults and has emerged as the most effective available intervention for pediatric rCDI. In this review, we synthesize current evidence on FMT efficacy, microbiome changes, and safety in children with CDI. Data from large multicenter cohort studies demonstrate cure rates exceeding 80%, with comparable efficacy observed in high-risk subpopulations, including those with inflammatory bowel disease and immunocompromising conditions. Critical research gaps remain, including the absence of randomized controlled trials and limited long-term safety data in children. Despite demonstrated effectiveness, significant barriers limit FMT accessibility for children. The COVID-19 pandemic has substantially impacted availability, and current regulatory changes have further restricted access. The lack of FDA-approved live biotherapeutic products approved for CDI in children creates additional challenges. We critically examine the emerging pipeline of next-generation microbiome-based therapies and identify the steps required to ensure children with recurrent CDI have access to safe, effective, and approved treatments.

Ampelopsis grossedentata restores the homeostasis of Th17/Treg cells to mitigate DSS-induced ulcerative colitis through TLR1-2/NF-κB signaling pathway. Journal of ethnopharmacology  |  2026-11-15

Ampelopsis grossedentata (AG), a traditional medicine from Southwest China, is frequently used to treat inflammation, anemopyretic cold, pharyngitis, hepatitis, and dysentery. Pharmacological studies confirmed that primary active constituents of AG, including flavonoids, profoundly affect anti-inflammatory processes and immunomodulation. However, the therapeutic effect and mechanisms of AG for ulcerative colitis (UC) are still unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the mechanisms of AG treatment of UC. UPLC-MS and network pharmacology were employed to identify the active components of AG and their potential anti-UC mechanisms. A 4% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced UC mouse model was established for assessing therapeutic efficacy of AG based on body weight, disease activity index, colon length, and histopathology of colon tissue. RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence assays and D-lactate levels were used to evaluate the inflammatory state and epithelial barrier integrity. Transcriptomic, proteomic, and flow cytometry analyses were performed to examine the underlying mechanisms. Molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), western blot and the TLR1/2 knockdown assays were used to identify and verify candidate targets. Five flavonoids in AG were characterized via UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS, with dihydromyricetin, taxifolin, myricitrin, and myricetin quantified by HPLC-DAD. Network pharmacology revealed 38 UC-related targets, enriched in IL-17/TNF signaling pathways. In vivo, AG alleviated UC-related symptoms, improved colon pathology, reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6), upregulated tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin 5), and inhibited the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (P65 and P50). Transcriptomic, proteomic, and flow cytometry analyses indicated that AG regulated Th17/Treg balance via the NF-κB signaling pathway. Structure-based molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, CETSA western blot and the TLR1/2 knockdown assays revealed that AG may act through the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer receptor. AG effectively attenuates DSS-induced UC, potentially by restoring Th17/Treg balance and inhibiting NF-κB signaling through the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer. AG is thus a therapeutic candidate for UC treatment.

Probiotics and Prebiotics in Restoring Gastrointestinal Microbiota Dysbiosis: Emerging Alternatives to Antibiotic Therapy.Review International journal of food science  |  2026-11-14

The human gastrointestinal (GI) microbiota is a complex and dynamic microbial ecosystem that plays a fundamental role in maintaining host metabolism, immune homeostasis, intestinal barrier integrity, and protection against pathogenic microorganisms. Disruption of this microbial community, known as gut dysbiosis, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous GI and systemic diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Clostridioides difficile infection, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, colorectal cancer, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. Although antibiotics remain indispensable for the treatment of bacterial infections, their indiscriminate use frequently disrupts gut microbial composition, reduces microbial diversity, promotes opportunistic pathogen overgrowth, and accelerates the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. These limitations have stimulated increasing interest in microbiota-targeted interventions as safer and more sustainable therapeutic strategies. Probiotics and prebiotics have emerged as promising approaches for restoring gut microbial homeostasis through complementary mechanisms. Probiotics inhibit pathogen colonization, enhance intestinal barrier function, produce antimicrobial metabolites, and modulate host immune responses. Prebiotics selectively stimulate the growth and metabolic activity of beneficial microorganisms, promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that support intestinal and systemic health. Increasing evidence from experimental studies, randomized clinical trials, and meta-analyses demonstrates that these microbiota-directed interventions can reduce antibiotic-associated diarrhea, improve symptoms of IBD and IBS, enhance Helicobacter pylori eradication, decrease recurrence of C. difficile infection, and contribute to the management of several metabolic disorders. Despite these promising findings, important challenges remain regarding strain specificity, optimal dosage, treatment duration, product standardization, regulatory oversight, and interindividual variability in treatment response. Emerging advances in metagenomics, metabolomics, artificial intelligence, and precision medicine are facilitating the development of personalized microbiome-based therapies that may further enhance clinical outcomes. This review critically summarizes the current understanding of GI microbiota dysbiosis, discusses the mechanisms through which probiotics and prebiotics restore microbial balance, evaluates their clinical applications as alternatives or adjuncts to antibiotic therapy, and highlights existing challenges and future research directions for microbiome-based therapeutics.

Prebiotic mannuronate oligosaccharides fortify the mucus barrier by binding host NLRP6 in a microbiota-independent manner.★ Cellular and molecular gastroenterology and hepatology  |  2026-11-14

The beneficial effects of prebiotics are conventionally attributed to their fermentation by the gut microbiota. Here, we uncover a direct, microbiota-independent mechanism by which mannuronate oligosaccharides (MAOS) fortify the mucus barrier in a structure-dependent manner. We demonstrate that MAOS functions as a direct host agonist by binding the NLRP6 pyrin domain at Arg87 with high affinity (KD = 0.9 μM). This binding event triggers NLRP6 inflammasome assembly and subsequent caspase-1 activation, leading to the formation of non-lytic GSDMD pores specifically at the apical membrane of goblet cells. These pores facilitate mucin 2 (Muc2) secretion, thereby enhancing mucosal defense and limiting bacterial adhesion. Our findings establish MAOS as a novel class of NLRP6 agonist that enhances mucosal barrier function through a GSDMD-dependent secretory mechanism. This work highlights that specific glycan structures, rather than microbial fermentation, can directly engage host immune receptors. This opens a new avenue for developing structure-guided glycan-based therapeutics that act directly on the host immune system to treat inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and other mucus barrier disorders.

With the increasing application of cadmium sulfide nanoparticles (CdS-NPs), their potential adverse effects on intestinal health have attracted growing attention. However, the impact of CdS-NPs exposure on inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that daily oral gavage exposure to 1 mg/kg CdS-NPs for 4 weeks or 11 weeks alone did not induce apparent colonic inflammation in healthy mice but exacerbated both acute and chronic DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by severe weight loss, increased disease activity, colon shortening, tissue damage, and immune infiltration. Integrated gut microbiota profiling and metabolomic analysis revealed that CdS-NPs markedly reduced the abundance and tryptophan-metabolizing capacity of Faecalibaculum rodentium (F. rodentium), leading to impaired indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) production. F. rodentium administration significantly alleviated CdS-NPs-aggravated DSS-induced colitis and restored serum ILA levels, accompanied by suppression of Th17 responses. Consistently, exogenous ILA treatment attenuated intestinal inflammation and suppressed Th17/IL-17 responses in vivo and inhibited Th17 differentiation in CD4+ T cells in vitro. Collectively, our findings identify microbial tryptophan metabolism and IL-17 signaling as critical mediators of intestinal inflammatory sensitization associated with CdS-NPs exposure and highlight the F. rodentium-ILA axis as a potential intervention target for CdS-NPs-induced intestinal inflammation.

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) stems from gut dysbiosis, impaired intestinal epithelial barriers and persistent mucosal immune dysfunction. Free oral probiotics or probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles yield poor therapeutic effects against IBD, as they degrade rapidly in the gastrointestinal tract and lack specific tropism for inflamed colon lesions. To overcome these delivery limitations, we fabricated pH-responsive hyaluronic acid (HA)-decorated alginate microcapsules (ALHA@LGG&EBMVs) to co-deliver viable Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) and extrusion-derived bacterial membrane vesicles (EBMVs). The electrosprayed Ca2+-crosslinked alginate skeleton confers robust gastric-acid protection, and surface-immobilized HA enables specific binding to CD44 overexpressed on inflamed colon epithelium. In vitro simulated gastric fluid assays revealed that the ALHA shell afforded superior protection to encapsulated LGG and EBMVs. In vivo therapeutic assays verified that the dual-cargo microcapsules mitigated colitis symptoms, restored epithelial tight junctions and reduced excessive intestinal epithelial permeability. Mechanistic studies revealed concurrent improvements across three key pathological dimensions: balancing the abnormal Th17/Treg ratio, promoting anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, and remodeling dysbiotic gut microbiota to expand beneficial commensals and reduce the abundance of pro-inflammatory pathobionts, which collectively facilitate the restoration of intestinal homeostasis. This polysaccharide delivery system integrates gastric-acid shielding and active inflammatory-lesion targeting to achieve multi-dimensional restoration of intestinal homeostasis. This proof-of-concept study offers a biocompatible microecological nanomedicine prototype for IBD intervention and establishes a blueprint for the development of oral gut-targeted biomaterials.

Dsg2 truncation causes a severe intestinal epithelial barrier breakdown and alters microbiome and immune response. JCI insight  |  2026-11-10

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) including Crohn’s disease (CD) are characterized by impaired intestinal epithelial barrier (IEB) function. Alterations in the desmosomal cadherin desmoglein 2 (Dsg2) have been linked to loss of IEB and intestinal inflammation in IBD. We investigated the pathogenic impact of a cytoplasmatic-truncating mutation in the desmosomal cadherin desmoglein 2 gene (DSG2) in an IBD patient. Genetic analysis of a CD patient revealed a likely pathogenic DSG2 mutation leading to a truncated protein lacking part of the intracellular domain. Generation of an enterocyte-specific mouse model to recapitulate the human mutation resulted in a lethal intestinal barrier defect which was evident in homozygous but not in heterozygous mice. In vitro, barrier defects correlated with changes in Dsg2 mobility and upregulation of pore-forming tight junction protein claudin-2. Organoids derived from heterozygous Dsg2 truncated mice exhibited increased epithelial susceptibility to proinflammatory cytokines. Bulk RNAseq followed by gene ontology analysis in homozygous mice revealed a skewed IL17/IL23 response overlapping with transcriptional changes in patients with CD. Furthermore, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed alterations in microbiome composition in animals that carried the Dsg2 truncation. In summary, a patient-derived mutation in the DSG2 gene causing cytoplasmic truncation in homozygous state induced a lethal intestinal barrier breakdown in mice paralleled by altered gut microbiota and intestinal immune response.

Microbiome changes following immigration: Rethinking “Westernization” as an explanatory concept.Review★ Gut microbes  |  2026-11-10

An emerging focus in microbiome science is on changes to people’s microbiomes following immigration, particularly to so-called Western countries, such as the USA, Canada, and Australia. Moving to these regions has been linked to microbiome changes that may increase immigrants’ susceptibility to health problems, including obesity and Inflammatory Bowel Disease. The term “Westernization” (and adjacent terminology) is frequently used to describe changes in diet, lifestyle, and built environment - and the consequent microbiome shifts - associated with immigration. However, what Westernization means in this context, and whether it is an appropriate descriptor, is often unclear. The purpose of this article is to provide a review of the diverse meanings associated with the terms “West”, “Western”, “Westernization” (and similar) across microbiome-related research and broader uses. We argue that these terms can obscure the specific structural, environmental, and cultural factors shaping microbiome changes. We discuss the consequences of this imprecision and provide recommendations for future research. Avoiding oversimplified conclusions about what impacts immigrants’ microbiome and health can contribute to more accurate targets for public health interventions for these groups in the long term.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation in the Treatment Strategy of Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Molecular Mechanisms, Clinical Evidence, and the Transition Toward Defined Microbial Therapeutics.Review International journal of molecular sciences  |  2026-11-08

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) comprises two clinically and pathologically distinct chronic intestinal inflammatory diseases, ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). Both diseases result from a dysregulated mucosal immune response to the commensal gut microbiota in genetically predisposed individuals. Because gut dysbiosis is a constant feature of both diseases, Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) has recently been investigated as a potential therapeutic strategy to restore a balanced microbiota composition in patients with IBD. There are five placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in UC and several meta-analyses, which showed that treatment with FMT using material from multiple donors can lead to clinical and endoscopic remission in patients with mild-to-moderate UC and may thus double to triple the rate of remission compared with placebo (number needed to treat [NNT] ≈ 3-5). In contrast, evidence for CD is limited and inconsistent, with one promising pilot study being contrasted with a large multicenter induction trial that was stopped for futility. This review provides an update of the current molecular understanding of FMT-induced recovery of mucosal barrier function and inflammation control in intestinal inflammation (α-diversity restoration and subsequent donor strain engraftment, production of short-chain fatty acids and secondary bile acids, tryptophan-AhR signaling, IL-10-mediated induction of Tregs vs. suppression of Th17 cells and restoration of epithelial barrier function). The review also summarizes the current clinical evidence for the use of FMT in IBD, its safety, and the current regulatory status. It also describes recent developments in FMT, including the use of defined bacterial consortia, ‘live biotherapeutic products’ (LBPs), and donor-recipient pairing. Collectively, FMT provides evidence of principle for manipulating intestinal microbial ecosystems to treat immune-mediated intestinal inflammation.

An engineered probiotic yeast converts extracellular ATP into adenosine to alleviate experimental colitis. Microbiological research  |  2026-11-07

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic relapsing intestinal disorder characterized by mucosal inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, and gut microbial dysbiosis. Excessive extracellular ATP (eATP) accumulates in inflamed intestinal tissues and amplifies immune activation through purinergic signaling, whereas adenosine generated from extracellular nucleotide metabolism exerts immunoregulatory effects. Here, we engineered the probiotic yeast Saccharomyces boulardii to sense eATP and convert it into adenosine through a CRISPR-engineered dual-enzyme pathway consisting of apyrase and 5’-nucleotidase. The engineered strain showed ATP-responsive pathway activation and efficiently generated adenosine from eATP, allowing extracellular nucleotide metabolism to be modulated in inflammatory settings. Oral administration of this strain significantly alleviated disease severity in acute dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and showed beneficial effects in a repeated-DSS model, improved barrier-related epithelial features, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and supported mucosal recovery. Integrated microbiome and transcriptome analyses further showed partial restoration of gut microbial community structure, partial recovery of predicted microbial purine-related pathways, and attenuation of mucosal T-cell activation. Together, these findings indicate that engineered Saccharomyces boulardii can regulate extracellular purine metabolism in the inflamed gut and provide a microbial strategy for modulating intestinal inflammation.
