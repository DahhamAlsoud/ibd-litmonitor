Sargassum soluble dietary fiber alleviates oxidative stress and gut microbiota dysbiosis to prevent inflammatory bowel disease in C57BL/6 mice. Journal of the science of food and agriculture  |  2026-10-14

Fermentation may enhance the bioactivity of Sargassum soluble dietary fiber (SDF). This study investigates the preventive effects of Bifidobacterium longum fermented Sargassum SDF (BL-SDF) against oxidative stress and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In vitro, the BL-SDF significantly reduced nitric oxide (NO) production by 16.98-23.04% and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation by 65.30-81.28% in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities, decreased malondialdehyde (MDA), and suppressed prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Concurrently, BL-SDF significantly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and interleukin-23 (IL-23). Mediterranean diet, gut microbiota, and intestinal barrier integrity: implications for digestive and systemic health.Review Antonie van Leeuwenhoek  |  2026-10-13

The Mediterranean diet has gained increasing attention for its effects on gut microbiota, microbial metabolism, intestinal barrier function, and related clinical outcomes. This narrative review critically synthesizes evidence from 51 publications identified through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar, including human intervention studies, observational studies, systematic and narrative reviews, and selected preclinical studies. The available evidence suggests that the MD can influence microbial composition and function, although taxonomic responses vary considerably between populations and study designs. More consistent findings involve microbial metabolic activity, particularly short-chain fatty acid production, bile acid remodeling, and polyphenol-derived metabolites. These changes may contribute to intestinal barrier regulation, mucosal homeostasis, and immune modulation. Human intervention studies provide supportive evidence in inflammatory bowel disease, functional gastrointestinal disorders, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and related conditions, while evidence for neurological, autoimmune, and cancer-related outcomes remains more limited and is often observational or mechanistic. Direct evidence for improved intestinal barrier function is still less extensive than indirect evidence based on inflammatory markers, microbial metabolites, and permeability-related measures. Differences in microbiome methodology, baseline diet, disease status, geographic background, and intervention duration contribute to variability across studies. The MD therefore represents a promising dietary framework for modulating gut microbial function and intestinal homeostasis, but further long-term and mechanistically integrated human studies are needed to define causal pathways and identify individuals most likely to benefit.

B cells regulate T cell transfer colitis by partially reconstituting luminal IgA and modulating the colonic microbiota.★ Inflammatory bowel diseases  |  2026-10-12

Loss of immune tolerance to commensal microbial antigens is a central driver of inflammatory bowel disease. While regulatory T cells are well recognized in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, the contribution of B cells-particularly IgA-secreting cells-remains incompletely defined. In the T cell transfer colitis model, cotransfer of splenic B cells attenuates disease, an effect largely attributed to IL-10-producing regulatory B cells (Bregs). However, alternative B cell-dependent mechanisms have not been fully explored. Here, we investigated whether splenic B cells regulate T cell transfer colitis through IgA-dependent mechanisms independent of Breg-associated cytokines. We show that splenic B cells from wild-type C57BL/6 (B6) mice reconstitute bone marrow and peripheral B cell compartments in RAG1⁻/⁻ recipients, undergo class switching, migrate to the colonic lamina propria as sIgA⁺ B lineage cells, and partially restore luminal secretory IgA. B cell cotransfer significantly attenuated clinical and histological colitis without increased expression of canonical Breg-associated cytokines, including interleukin-10, transforming growth factor β, and interleukin-35 subunits. In contrast, splenic B cells from IgA-deficient mice failed to attenuate colitis despite comparable peripheral B cell reconstitution. Restoration of secretory IgA was associated with increased bacterial IgA coating, reduced fecal bacterial burden, and remodeling of the colonic microbiota, including enrichment of beneficial commensal taxa. Importantly, because IgA-deficient B cells failed to attenuate colitis despite comparable peripheral B cell reconstitution, these findings support an important role for IgA in mediating the protective effect of B cell transfer. Collectively, these findings identify IgA-mediated modulation of the microbiota as an alternate mechanism by which B cells attenuate T cell transfer colitis, highlighting a Breg-independent regulatory pathway with potential relevance to inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis.

In summary, the unique properties of β-glucan offer exciting new opportunities for targeted oral drug delivery.

Pro-inflammatory dietary patterns are associated with an increased risk of Crohn’s disease (CD) and shifts in the gut microbiota. Our previous multi-omics analysis had identified the enrichment of Morganella morganii (M. morganii) in both patients with CD and individuals consuming pro-inflammatory diets. In this study, we aimed to investigate the role and potential mechanisms by which M. morganii contributes to intestinal inflammation. Using murine models of colitis, mouse colon organoids, and epithelial cell systems, combined with metabolomic profiling and pharmacological interventions, we investigated the effects of M. morganii and its secreted metabolites on tight junction integrity and intestinal permeability. M. morganii aggravated colitis severity and induced a remarkable increase in intestinal permeability with disruption of tight junction architecture, including reduced occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) expression and ultrastructural damage. Metabolomic analysis of the <3-kDa fraction of M. morganii culture supernatant identified histamine as a key bioactive molecule. Histamine recapitulated barrier disruption and colitis exacerbation in vivo. Pharmacological studies suggested that histamine-induced injury is mediated mainly through the histamine receptor H4 (HRH4) and that the inhibition of histamine synthesis significantly restored barrier integrity and alleviated colitis. These findings suggest a previously unrecognized pathway in which the diet-responsive pathobiont M. morganii may impair epithelial barrier integrity through histamine-HRH4 signaling. Targeting histamine synthesis and HRH4 may offer a potential therapeutic avenue worth further investigation.

Gastrointestinal-Responsive Jelly Fig-Alginate Microgels for Restoring Intestinal Barrier Homeostasis through Microbiota-Metabolite Remodeling.★ ACS applied materials & interfaces  |  2026-10-07

Oral biomaterials capable of protecting natural bioactives during gastrointestinal transit while restoring intestinal barrier function offer a promising strategy for inflammatory bowel disease intervention. Here, we developed edible Jelly Fig (Ficus awkeotsang Makino, JF) pectin-alginate composite microgels (Mgel) through Ca2+-mediated ionic crosslinking as a gastrointestinal-responsive oral biomaterial. The resulting microgels exhibited uniform spherical morphology, enhanced mechanical robustness, gastric stability, and intestinal responsiveness, thereby improving the delivery performance of JF-derived bioactive components. Phytochemical and HPLC analyses showed that the ethanol-soluble fraction of JF was enriched in phenolic constituents and displayed potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and epithelial barrier-protective effects in macrophage and intestinal epithelial cell models. In dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis mice, orally administered Mgel showed superior protective efficacy compared with unformulated JF hydrogel at an equivalent dose, as evidenced by reduced body weight loss, disease activity, colon shortening, histological injury, inflammatory cytokine production, oxidative stress, and intestinal permeability. Mgel further restored mucus and tight junction barrier markers, promoted epithelial regeneration, and improved mucosal healing. Moreover, 16S rRNA sequencing combined with targeted metabolite profiling revealed that Mgel remodeled the gut microbiota, enriched beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, and restored short-chain fatty acid and secondary bile acid metabolism. These findings demonstrate that Jelly Fig-alginate microgels serve as a food-grade, gastrointestinal-responsive oral biomaterial platform for coordinated restoration of epithelial barrier integrity and microbiota-metabolite homeostasis in colitis.

Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis are both classified as forms of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD). Strategies that reduce overall systemic exposure while delivering antiinflammatory drug directly to the inflamed colon are necessary for treating IBD. Flurbiprofen is an effective Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug (NSAID) with potential utility in IBD because it inhibits cyclooxygenase-1 and cyclooxygenase-2, thereby reducing the synthesis of proinflammatory prostaglandins. However, its clinical use for IBD may be limited by gastrointestinal toxicity and the inadequate delivery of conventional oral formulations to the inflamed colonic mucosa. These limitations may result in insufficient drug concentrations at the target site while increasing systemic exposure and the risk of adverse effects. The current status of colon-targeted drug delivery has improved recently. Key considerations include current formulation approaches, colon-targeting strategies, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variables, interspecies differences in intestinal microbiota, stability concerns, and the challenges of translating preclinical findings to humans. Changes to gut microbiomes are linked to inflammatory bowel disease, but little is known about how microbial change is modulated in complex gut microbiota systems. Here, we address this issue by revealing the influence of microbial interactions on gut microbiota response to ulcerative colitis (UC) using a spouse-pair cohort design composed of an UC patient and his healthy spouse living in the same household environment. We calculate the difference in abundance of a microbial species between each pair of spouses as a proxy of UC-induced phenotypic plasticity (uPP). We classify all microbes into functionally distinct modules based on the similarity of uPP across household environments. We find that a marked increase in microbial plasticity is mediated by profound restructuring of microbial interactomes in the microbial communities. By inferring multilayer interactome networks, our model positions phenotypic plasticity as a systematic mechanistic force of UC, paving the way for its ecology-informed diagnostics and interventions.

Research progress on intestinal probiotics-mediated protection of the intestinal mucosal barrier in ulcerative colitis.Review Frontiers in immunology  |  2026-09-28

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a major subtype of inflammatory bowel disease characterized by a chronic, relapsing course. Its pathogenesis involves complex interactions among gut microbial dysbiosis, epithelial barrier disruption, dysregulated immune responses, and oxidative stress. Impairment of the intestinal mucosal barrier facilitates the translocation of luminal antigens, bacteria, and microbial metabolites into the mucosa, thereby aggravating local inflammation. Probiotics may help maintain barrier homeostasis by reshaping the gut microbial community, reinforcing epithelial and mucus barriers, modulating mucosal immunity, and reducing oxidative injury. This review summarizes recent evidence on the protective effects of probiotics, including Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., and Akkermansia muciniphila, on the intestinal mucosal barrier in UC. Current findings suggest that probiotics can inhibit pathogen adhesion and colonization, enhance the production of beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan-derived compounds, and promote the expression of tight-junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1, and claudin-4, as well as mucins such as MUC1 and MUC2. These effects may be mediated through the regulation of NF-κB, NLRP3, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and related signaling pathways, thereby attenuating inflammation and oxidative stress and promoting epithelial repair. Combination strategies involving probiotics and prebiotics, traditional Chinese medicine-derived components, postbiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, or novel delivery systems may improve microbial engraftment, enhance colon-targeted delivery, and strengthen microbiota remodeling and barrier repair. Nevertheless, the available evidence remains limited by substantial strain specificity, insufficient clinical validation, and the absence of standardized intervention protocols. Further multi-omics studies and well-designed clinical trials are warranted to clarify the efficacy, mechanisms, optimal dosing, and safety of these interventions in UC.
