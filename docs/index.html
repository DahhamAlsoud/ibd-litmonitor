Fecal Microbiota Transfer for Recurrent Clostridioides difficile Infection: A Retrospective Single-Center Analysis. Zeitschrift fur Gastroenterologie  |  2026-10-16

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is the leading cause of healthcare-associated infectious diarrhea. Despite high initial response rates to antibiotic therapy, recurrence occurs in approximately 20% of patients. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as an effective treatment option for multiple recurrences, demonstrating high efficacy and a favorable safety profile. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of FMT in patients with recurrent CDI (rCDI). In this single-center retrospective study, 75 patients who underwent FMT for rCDI between November 2016 and January 2026 after experiencing at least two recurrences despite standard antibiotic therapy were included. Donor screening was performed according to the requirements of the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM). Following CDI-directed antibiotic pretreatment, FMT was administered either by colonoscopy, oral capsules, or a combination of both. Patients were followed for three months. The primary endpoint was the absence of diarrhea as a surrogate marker for rCDI, including a predefined subgroup analysis of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Follow-up data were available for 65 patients, of whom 56 (86.1%) achieved sustained clinical remission. Patients with concomitant IBD (n=15) showed a comparable response rate (86.7%). The route of FMT administration had no significant impact on treatment outcome. Overall, FMT was well tolerated, with only four patients (5.3%) reporting predominantly self-limiting adverse events. FMT is a highly effective and safe treatment for recurrent CDI, including in multimorbid patients and those with concomitant IBD. Spontaneous hypopigmentation mutant in threespine stickleback fish exhibits changes in the gut microbiome consistent with inflammatory bowel diseases. G3 (Bethesda, Md.)  |  2026-10-15

Albinism is a large group of disorders characterized by a loss of pigment in ectoderm-derived tissues - most noticeably impacting the skin, hair, and eyes. Albinism is categorized as either syndromic or non-syndromic. Syndromic albinisms include a wide range of disorders with mutations impacting not just pigmentation but other cellular functions. These include, non-exclusively, melanogenesis disorders, endosome trafficking disorders like lysosomal storage disorders, and cell migration disorders. Mutations leading to syndromic albinisms can cause multi-organ system symptoms due to broad disruption lysosome related organelles (LROs) function. Various genetic mutations impact biogenesis, cell maturation, or trafficking at different stages which can lead to a range of severity. Syndromic albinisms also present with different combinations of symptoms depending on the impacted organelles; including oculocutaneous albinism (OCA) (melanosomes), blood diathesis (platelet dense granules), pulmonary fibrosis (lamellar bodies), or chronic inflammation (multiple cellular sources). Animal models for these multisymptomatic complex disorders are needed to understand the effects on systemic health. One such area of interest is in gut health of those with syndromic albinism. Shared inflammatory patterns of syndromic albinisms like Hermansky-Pudlak Syndromes (HPS) and Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) are well studied, with animal models of HPS jointly used to study patterns of inflammation in IBD. However, the impact of this shared gastrointestinal inflammation on the gut microbiome remains unexplored. Using a spontaneously identified mutant family of threespine stickleback fish, we characterize phenotypes consistent with oculocutaneous albinism and vision impairment and demonstrate shared features of gut health to IBD extend from inflammation to the gut microbiome. We argue syndromic albinism models should be explored as models to study the gut microbiome in addition to inflammatory phenotypes in IBD, and importantly, be used to test microbial/dietary interventions for both IBD and syndromic albinisms.

Functionally declining microbiota in chronic disease: beyond taxonomic loss.Review★ Gut microbes  |  2026-10-15

Microbiota deterioration in modern human populations has been linked to chronic disease, and taxonomic profiling is increasingly used to understand, diagnose, and guide treatment. Yet such profiles do not fully capture disease-relevant microbiota states. I propose functional decline within retained taxa as a distinct axis of microbiota deterioration, complementary to taxonomic loss. Modern environmental pressures can drive not only taxonomic loss but also directional selection toward variants or lineages with reduced host-beneficial function or increased harmful activity. Two mechanistically characterized systems from studies by my group illustrate this process: antibiotic-selected Akkermansia variants with diminished metabolic benefits, and Faecalibacterium pathobionts that promote disease when dominant over beneficial strains, with supporting evidence from atopic dermatitis and pediatric inflammatory bowel disease. Evidence from additional gut taxa suggests that this pattern may extend beyond these two examples. By integrating this hidden axis of functional decline with the well-established axis of taxonomic loss, the proposed two-axis framework could help clarify inconsistent microbiota-disease associations and guide strain-resolved, function-first approaches to microbiota analysis and intervention.

Gut Microbiota-Dependent Tryptophan Metabolism Drives the Therapeutic Effects of Baitouweng Tang in Colitis via the AhR Pathway. Food science & nutrition  |  2026-10-14

With dysregulation of the gut microbiota and its metabolic byproducts playing a pivotal role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) pathogenesis. Baitouweng Tang (BTWT), a traditional herbal formulation, has long been employed to treat gastrointestinal disorders and shows promising potential in the management of IBD. However, its precise pharmacological mechanisms remain elusive. In this study, we employed targeted metabolomics, high-throughput sequencing, and molecular biology techniques to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of BTWT in the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC). BTWT significantly enhanced the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium, improved the indole metabolic pathway of tryptophan, and restored the levels of indole metabolites, such as indole-3-lactic acid (ILA). Furthermore, in an in vitro lipoteichoic acid (LTA)-induced HT-29 inflammation model, ILA treatment attenuated inflammation and concurrently increased the expression of AhR, CYP1A1, and CYP1B1. These findings suggest a potential link between BTWT-induced changes in tryptophan metabolites, AhR pathway activation, and the amelioration of colitis, though the causal relationship remains to be established. Our study provides a basis for further mechanistic investigation into the AhR pathway as a possible therapeutic target in UC.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), yet how specific microbial components influence disease progression remains incompletely understood. Our study reveals that decreased abundance of Lactobacillus species correlates with ulcerative colitis severity in patients. Among tested strains, L. plantarum A736, isolated from traditional fermented foods, demonstrated protective effects in ameliorating dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in a mouse model. Strikingly, multi-omics analysis revealed that L. plantarum A736 uniquely enhances choline acetyltransferase-expressing (ChAT+) neurons in the enteric nervous system and elevates acetylcholine (ACh) levels. Crucially, selective ablation of colonic ChAT+ neurons abolished the protective effect of both L. plantarum A736 and ILA, establishing their essential role in mediating these effects. These findings demonstrate that microbial metabolites can modulate enteric neuronal circuits to regulate gut immunity. The identified ILA-ChAT+ neuron axis represents a novel neuro-immune pathway that could be targeted for IBD treatment.

Mediterranean diet, gut microbiota, and intestinal barrier integrity: implications for digestive and systemic health.Review Antonie van Leeuwenhoek  |  2026-10-13

The Mediterranean diet has gained increasing attention for its effects on gut microbiota, microbial metabolism, intestinal barrier function, and related clinical outcomes. This narrative review critically synthesizes evidence from 51 publications identified through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar, including human intervention studies, observational studies, systematic and narrative reviews, and selected preclinical studies. The available evidence suggests that the MD can influence microbial composition and function, although taxonomic responses vary considerably between populations and study designs. More consistent findings involve microbial metabolic activity, particularly short-chain fatty acid production, bile acid remodeling, and polyphenol-derived metabolites. These changes may contribute to intestinal barrier regulation, mucosal homeostasis, and immune modulation. Human intervention studies provide supportive evidence in inflammatory bowel disease, functional gastrointestinal disorders, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and related conditions, while evidence for neurological, autoimmune, and cancer-related outcomes remains more limited and is often observational or mechanistic. Direct evidence for improved intestinal barrier function is still less extensive than indirect evidence based on inflammatory markers, microbial metabolites, and permeability-related measures. Differences in microbiome methodology, baseline diet, disease status, geographic background, and intervention duration contribute to variability across studies. The MD therefore represents a promising dietary framework for modulating gut microbial function and intestinal homeostasis, but further long-term and mechanistically integrated human studies are needed to define causal pathways and identify individuals most likely to benefit.

B cells regulate T cell transfer colitis by partially reconstituting luminal IgA and modulating the colonic microbiota.★ Inflammatory bowel diseases  |  2026-10-12

Loss of immune tolerance to commensal microbial antigens is a central driver of inflammatory bowel disease. While regulatory T cells are well recognized in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, the contribution of B cells-particularly IgA-secreting cells-remains incompletely defined. In the T cell transfer colitis model, cotransfer of splenic B cells attenuates disease, an effect largely attributed to IL-10-producing regulatory B cells (Bregs). However, alternative B cell-dependent mechanisms have not been fully explored. Here, we investigated whether splenic B cells regulate T cell transfer colitis through IgA-dependent mechanisms independent of Breg-associated cytokines. We show that splenic B cells from wild-type C57BL/6 (B6) mice reconstitute bone marrow and peripheral B cell compartments in RAG1⁻/⁻ recipients, undergo class switching, migrate to the colonic lamina propria as sIgA⁺ B lineage cells, and partially restore luminal secretory IgA. B cell cotransfer significantly attenuated clinical and histological colitis without increased expression of canonical Breg-associated cytokines, including interleukin-10, transforming growth factor β, and interleukin-35 subunits. In contrast, splenic B cells from IgA-deficient mice failed to attenuate colitis despite comparable peripheral B cell reconstitution. Restoration of secretory IgA was associated with increased bacterial IgA coating, reduced fecal bacterial burden, and remodeling of the colonic microbiota, including enrichment of beneficial commensal taxa. Importantly, because IgA-deficient B cells failed to attenuate colitis despite comparable peripheral B cell reconstitution, these findings support an important role for IgA in mediating the protective effect of B cell transfer. Collectively, these findings identify IgA-mediated modulation of the microbiota as an alternate mechanism by which B cells attenuate T cell transfer colitis, highlighting a Breg-independent regulatory pathway with potential relevance to inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis.

The microbiota-metabolism-immunity axis provides a coherent framework for interpreting the multi-level actions of TCM-derived components, but their causal mechanisms and clinical relevance to human CAC require further validation.

Current treatments for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) lack real-time inflammatory monitoring and lesion-specific drug delivery, resulting in poor efficacy and systemic toxicity. To address this, we present a smart oral capsule that operates a closed-loop “sense-decide-act” strategy entirely autonomously-sensing and drug release are driven by changes in the digestive tract environment without any external intervention. The capsule features a dual-layer shell sequentially responsive to pH and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), enabling colon-targeted delivery and inflammation-specific activation. An integrated multimodal sensor wirelessly tracks colonic TNF-α (>5 ng mL-1), pH (>7.0), and Na+ (>100 mM). The encapsulated budesonide nanoparticles (Bud@PEG-SS-NPs) release the drug specifically in reactive oxygen species (ROS)-rich microenvironments and show superior anti-inflammatory efficacy in both lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cellular models and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced mouse colitis. In live rats, the capsule achieved on-demand drug release in response to elevated local TNF-α levels, and a single intervention significantly alleviated inflammation. This work establishes a new paradigm for personalized, adaptive IBD therapy and provides a versatile platform for closed-loop management of other gastrointestinal diseases.

A Review of Detection Probes for Vanin-1: Development and Biological Applications in Various Inflammatory Diseases.Systematic review Chemistry & biodiversity  |  2026-10

Vascular non-inflammatory molecule-1 (Vanin-1), also known as pantetheinase, is a key enzyme that regulates oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and metabolic processes by catalyzing the hydrolysis of pantetheine into cysteamine and pantothenic acid. Recent studies have progressively elucidated its pivotal role in various pathological processes, including acute kidney injury (AKI), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), liver injury, and rheumatoid arthritis (RA). As an important biomarker for early diagnosis and assessment of treatment efficacy, it has attracted widespread attention and driven the rapid development of highly sensitive and specific detection tools. This article provides a systematic review of Vanin-1 probes developed in recent years, covering fluorescent, chemiluminescent, bioluminescent, and electrochemical sensors, with a focus on their molecular design principles, detection mechanisms, performance characteristics, and biological applications. In addition, this article outlines future trends in Vanin-1 probe technologies, providing a comprehensive theoretical and methodological reference to support further basic research and clinical translation applications related to Vanin-1.

Intestinal T-cell migration: unraveling mechanisms and therapeutic implications.Review Journal of advanced research  |  2026-10-10

Intestinal T-cell migration links mucosal immunity with systemic immune homeostasis and contributes to immune surveillance, barrier defense, and peripheral tissue inflammation. Advances in single-cell omics, spatial analysis, and intravital imaging are reshaping our understanding of its subset specificity and tissue selectivity. This review summarizes the molecular mechanisms, microenvironmental regulation, pathological significance along the gut-organ axes, and related therapeutic strategies of intestinal T-cell migration, with a particular focus on how migratory imbalance contributes to intestinal and extraintestinal immune-mediated diseases. Intestinal T-cell migration is jointly driven by gut imprinting, S1P-S1PR-mediated egress, chemokine-guided trafficking, integrin-dependent adhesion, and cytoskeletal remodeling, and is further regulated by metabolic, epigenetic, and microbial signals. This process is highly dependent on T-cell subset and tissue context: it promotes effector T-cell recruitment and inflammatory amplification in inflammatory bowel disease, manifests as restricted antitumor T-cell infiltration in colorectal cancer, and may contribute to distant tissue injury through the gut-organ axes. Existing S1PR modulators, integrin antagonists, and chemokine-targeted strategies have demonstrated the therapeutic tractability of migratory pathways; however, pathway redundancy, tissue heterogeneity, and impairment of protective immunity continue to limit their application. Future strategies should move beyond broad suppression of migration toward subset-, tissue-, and disease stage-specific precision modulation, combined with microbiota-based interventions and biomarker-guided stratification to improve therapeutic efficacy.

Sun Y, Liu Y, Xie L, Zhu L, Peng W

Phase 2 Trial of Icotrokinra for Patients with Ulcerative Colitis.RCT★ The New England journal of medicine  |  2026-10

Monoclonal antibodies targeting the interleukin-23 pathway are effective in treating inflammatory bowel disease. However, these therapies must be administered intravenously or by subcutaneous injection. We conducted a phase 2b, double-blind, randomized trial of oral icotrokinra, a peptide that blocks the interleukin-23 receptor, in adults with moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis. Participants were assigned to receive icotrokinra at a once-daily dose of 400 mg, 200 mg, or 100 mg or placebo. The primary end point was clinical response at week 12. Secondary end points included clinical remission, remission of symptoms, and endoscopic improvement at week 12. A total of 252 participants underwent randomization: 63 were assigned to the 400-mg group, 62 to the 200-mg group, 64 to the 100-mg group, and 63 to the placebo group. All the doses of icotrokinra were superior to placebo with respect to a clinical response at week 12, which occurred in 63% of the participants in the 400-mg group, 58% in the 200-mg group, and 55% in the 100-mg group, as compared with 27% in the placebo group (P<0.001 for all comparisons with placebo). The percentage of participants with clinical remission at week 12 was also significantly higher in the 400-mg group than in the placebo group (30% vs. 11%, P = 0.006), as were the percentage of participants with symptomatic remission (46% vs. 19%, P<0.001) and the percentage of participants with endoscopic improvement (37% vs. 14%, P = 0.002). Clinical remission at week 12 occurred in 24% of the participants in the 200-mg group (P = 0.054 for the comparison with placebo); in accordance with the fixed-sequence testing procedure to account for multiple comparisons, no further formal testing of secondary end points was conducted. Through week 28, the percentage of participants with adverse events was 60% in the 400-mg group, 66% in the 200-mg group, 66% in the 100-mg group, and 62% in the placebo group. For serious adverse events, the percentages were 2%, 5%, 0%, and 10%, respectively. Among participants with moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis, a clinical response at week 12 was significantly more common with once-daily oral icotrokinra at each of three tested doses than with placebo.
